About this tool

This site gathers past military discharge upgrade decisions from official government sources and makes them searchable in one place. Every result links to the official copy of the decision.

Not legal advice

This is a research tool. It does not give legal advice, and past decisions do not predict the outcome of any individual case. Each case turns on its own records and evidence. Talk to a lawyer or accredited representative about your situation.

About "auto-extracted" information

The outcome, the discharge type before and after, and the issue tags are read from each decision by a computer program. It is usually right but can make mistakes, especially for documents with scanned pages. The official decision always governs. Tags only look at the facts of each case, not the standard legal paragraphs every decision repeats. For example, a "PTSD" tag means PTSD came up in that case.

Plain-language guides

Discharge Review Board vs. Board for Correction

Each military department has two kinds of boards that can upgrade a discharge:

  • Discharge Review Board (DRB). Reviews administrative discharges. You must apply within 15 years of your discharge (form DD 293). The DRB can change the characterization of service and the narrative reason. It cannot change a discharge given by a general court-martial. You can ask for a records review or a personal appearance hearing.
  • Board for Correction of Military (or Naval) Records (BCMR / BCNR). Can correct any error or injustice in a military record, including a discharge (form DD 149). It's the route after the 15-year DRB window closes, or after the DRB says no. There is a 3-year filing deadline from when you discovered the error, but boards often waive it "in the interest of justice."
  • Discharge Appeal Review Board (DARB). A Defense Department-level board, established in 2024, for a final review after a service DRB denies an upgrade.

The 15-year rule

Discharge Review Boards can only hear cases filed within 15 years of the date of discharge. After that, the Board for Correction of Military/Naval Records is the place to apply.

What "granted in part" means

The board gave some, but not all, of the relief asked for. For example, it changed the narrative reason or reentry code but did not upgrade the characterization of service. In this tool, "Granted in part" means the board granted relief but the characterization stayed the same. An upgrade that's smaller than what was requested (say, Other Than Honorable to General when Honorable was asked for) shows as "Granted," with the before → after discharge types, so check the decision for details.

Liberal consideration

Since 2014, a series of Defense Department memos has told the boards to give "liberal consideration" to veterans whose discharges may have been affected by mental health conditions or experiences:

  • Hagel memo (2014): PTSD.
  • Kurta memo (2017): expanded to all mental health conditions, including TBI, and to sexual assault and sexual harassment.
  • Wilkie memo (2018): guidance on equity, injustice and clemency, including weight for post-service conduct.

Congress has also written liberal consideration into the laws governing these boards (10 U.S.C. §§ 1552 and 1553). Under the Kurta memo, boards ask four questions:

  1. Did the veteran have a condition or experience that may excuse or mitigate the discharge?
  2. Did that condition exist, or that experience occur, during military service?
  3. Does that condition or experience actually excuse or mitigate the discharge?
  4. Does that condition or experience outweigh the discharge?

Army decisions answer these questions in writing, and this tool turns the answers into tags you can filter on: "Board found mitigating condition" (question 1), and "Condition outweighed discharge" or "Condition did not outweigh discharge" (question 4).

Privacy

This site uses only public decisions that the government has already redacted. It does not record what you search for and does not try to identify anyone in a decision.
